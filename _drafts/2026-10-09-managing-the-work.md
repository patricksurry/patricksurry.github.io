In the [last post]({% post_url 2026-10-09-work-and-working %}) I argued for separating the work from the working: keep products as artifacts, run the process as a graph of task contracts, and give LLMs the linear pieces. Once agents are first-class workers in that graph, the question shifts from how to prompt them to how to manage them. We've been working out how to manage people for more than a century, and the lessons carry over.

Early management theory was obsessed with process. At Bethlehem Steel in the 1890s, Frederick Taylor timed shovelers with a stopwatch and worked out that the ideal load was about 21 pounds. *The Principles of Scientific Management* (1911) turned that into a method: find the one best way to do each job, then make sure everyone does it that way. That works for shoveling, but not for knowledge work. The manager can't know the one best way, and trying to supervise every step buries them. In *The Practice of Management* (1954), Peter Drucker argued for management by objectives: agree on the outcome a person owns, then judge the result, not the motions. Militaries got there earlier. The Prussian idea of *Auftragstaktik*, or mission command, gives subordinates the commander's intent and constraints and leaves the method to the people on the ground, because they can see what the commander can't.

Agent orchestration is now replaying that history, and so far it's mostly landed on Taylor. [MetaGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.00352) builds the org chart into the framework: product manager, architect, and engineer personas following standard operating procedures, summed up as "Code = SOP(Team)." Steve Yegge's [Gas Town](https://steve-yegge.medium.com/gas-town-emergency-user-manual-cf0e4556d74b) runs dozens of coding agents as a factory. A Mayor takes your orders, disposable "polecats" each grind through a single task and vanish, and a Witness watches over them and unsticks them. Day to day, most of us do the same thing by hand. We watch the transcript scroll, approve each step, and correct each move. Guides to human-in-the-loop design now warn about *approval fatigue*: gate everything and approval becomes a reflex, so the oversight exists only on paper. It's Taylor's stopwatch, held by a supervisor who can't keep up.

There are good reasons for it. Agents are new, we don't yet know what they'll do, and assembly lines are easier to build than judgment. But the interesting signals point the other way. Gas Town's most durable piece may be its work ledger, a persistent graph of tasks that outlives any one agent's session. And when Maggie Appleton [took it apart](https://maggieappleton.com/gastown), she found that the bottleneck moves to the human: it churns through plans so fast that "you have to do a LOT of design and planning to keep the engine fed." That's Drucker's point. Once the doing is cheap, the manager's job is deciding what to build.

A good team lead works differently. Trust the people and trust the process, and you can ignore the process and focus on the product. The lead's own work is strategy, planning, review, feedback and direction. They decide what to build, break it into tasks with clear contracts, judge what comes back, and say what to do differently. Workers, human or agent, are trusted to follow institutional procedure. And that procedure is itself a product: handbooks, playbooks and style guides distilled from past work. Trust is earned, not assumed. In *High Output Management*, Andy Grove called this *task-relevant maturity*. Someone new to a kind of task needs close, structured supervision, while someone who has done it many times needs only the goal. Agents move along the same curve, one kind of task at a time. The far end of the curve is what 37signals called [managers of one](https://signalvnoise.com/posts/1430-hire-managers-of-one): people who "come up with their own goals and execute them." Few agents are there yet, but that's the direction to manage toward, not away from.

Trust still needs checking. The answer isn't to read transcripts, it's core sampling. Geologists don't excavate a whole site; they drill a narrow core and read the layers. Every so often, pick a finished task and drill all the way down. Why did you do it that way? Why that assumption? Why that source? Toyota's "five whys" is the same instinct: keep asking until you hit either solid ground or a cause worth fixing. Most cores come up clean, and that's the evidence that earns trust. When one doesn't, the fix usually belongs in the playbook the task was following rather than in the task itself, so the lesson reaches all future work. Sampling matters more for agents than for people, not less. A person can be held accountable for their work; an agent can't, so the accountability stays with whoever delegated.

Sampling only works if there's something to drill into, and that's where the graph earns its keep. Each task node carries a summary of what was done and why, and that's what the manager reads day to day. Below it sits the full transcript, which nobody reads unless they're drilling a core. For agent-worked tasks that record is complete, so a core can go all the way down.

Sampling is also a reminder that process is nested. The manager's process isn't the worker's. My graph has nodes like "decide what to build," "plan the release," and "review the draft." A worker's graph sits inside one of my nodes, and to me it's a single step with a contract and a result. The manager's product is the plan, the review, and the direction; the worker's product is the code or the report. Each level works its own graph and treats the level below as a set of contracts, not a stream of activity. Getting tangled in someone else's transcript is a sign you've slipped down a level and are doing their job instead of yours. That's also why the much-hyped "team of one" isn't flat. One person directing a hundred agents still runs a hierarchy, with a single manager at the top.

Management theory spent a century learning that you scale by delegating outcomes, not supervising motions. Agents don't change that lesson. They make it urgent, because they produce working faster than anyone can watch it. Manage the work, sample the working, and fix the playbooks rather than the people.
